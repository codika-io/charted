Chemistry occupies the middle ground between physics and biology. It works at the scale of atoms and molecules, where quantum mechanics determines bonding and reactivity, but its frame of reference is the everyday: how reactions proceed in solution, how matter is identified and quantified, how new substances are designed and made. The discipline organises itself by what it operates on and what it builds. *Analytical chemistry* asks what is present and at what concentration. *Organic chemistry* studies the carbon-based molecules that make up most of the matter chemists synthesise, and the reactions that transform one into another. *Inorganic chemistry* extends the same question to the rest of the periodic table, with particular attention to coordination complexes, organometallics, and solid-state materials. *Physical chemistry* derives the rate laws, equilibria, and spectroscopic signatures that the other branches rely on, anchored in thermodynamics and quantum mechanics. *Biochemistry* turns the same toolkit on the molecules of life. *Materials chemistry* sits at the engineering frontier, where the goal is no longer to understand a molecule but to design one with a specified function.

Methodological progress in twenty-first-century chemistry comes from three directions: instrumentation that resolves structure and dynamics at increasingly fine scales (cryo-electron microscopy, ultrafast spectroscopy, single-molecule detection), computation that predicts reactivity from first principles (density functional theory, ab initio molecular dynamics, machine-learned interatomic potentials), and synthetic strategies that compose known reactions into deliberate routes to specified products. The topic pages under this branch each focus on a frontier where one or more of these methodological strands is producing a recognisable cluster of recent papers.
